ENGLAND AND WALES RESIDENTIAL LEASE RISK GUIDANCE

Classify issues carefully.

High Risk:
- A clause allows lockout, seizure of belongings, utility shutoff, or eviction without court/legal process.
- A clause shifts all structural, exterior, sanitation, heating, water, gas, or electrical-installation repair obligations to the tenant in a way that may conflict with Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 - Section 11.
- A clause attempts to exclude habitability obligations or makes the tenant responsible for serious pre-existing housing-condition defects.
- A tenancy deposit clause appears to omit deposit protection requirements for an assured shorthold tenancy.
- A clause imposes a prohibited or excessive payment, fee, or deposit under Tenant Fees Act 2019 - Schedule 1.
- A clause is heavily one-sided and may create a significant imbalance under Consumer Rights Act 2015 - Section 62.

Medium Risk:
- The clause has unresolved placeholders such as names, dates, rent amount, deposit amount, property address, or city.
- The lease duration is unclear.
- The rent, payment date, payment method, or late-payment consequences are unclear.
- The deposit clause lacks return timing, deduction standards, inventory procedure, or dispute process.
- The landlord access clause lacks notice requirements or reasonable access limits.
- The repair clause does not distinguish landlord statutory repairs, tenant minor repairs, tenant-caused damage, and ordinary wear and tear.
- The termination clause lacks notice period, grounds, or consequences.
- The jurisdiction or dispute-resolution clause is blank or unclear.

Low Risk:
- The clause is standard, specific, and does not create a legal or practical concern based on the provided legal reference.

Important output rule:
Do not call a clause illegal unless a cited legal source supports that conclusion. For template blanks, use Missing Required Information. For practical drafting concerns, explain that the issue is ambiguity or practical risk rather than legal invalidity.

Deposit guidance:
For deposit clauses, check whether the clause mentions protection in an authorised tenancy deposit scheme, return timing, permitted deductions, inventory/check-out evidence, and dispute handling. If the tenancy type is unclear, say applicability is uncertain.

Repair guidance:
For repair clauses, do not treat ordinary tenant minor-repair duties as unlawful. Flag only if the clause appears to transfer statutory landlord obligations for structure, exterior, sanitation, heating, water, gas, or electrical installations entirely to the tenant.

Fees guidance:
For fees, distinguish ordinary rent/utilities/council tax from administrative fees, cleaning fees, renewal fees, excessive default charges, or unusually high deposits.

Professional-cleaning assessment — England

- A requirement to hire professional cleaners or provide professional-cleaning receipts should trigger review for a potential third-party service requirement under Tenant Fees Act 2019 Section 1(3).
- Explain that requirement explicitly. A vague cleaning standard is a separate drafting concern and must not replace the substantive explanation.
- An obligation to return the property to its original standard of cleanliness, allowing for fair wear and tear, is not by itself a requirement to hire a third party.
- Distinguish mandatory cleaning services or fees from damages claimed for an actual breach. Assess the latter using Schedule 1 paragraph 5 and the available facts.
- Do not use Landlord and Tenant Act 1985 Section 11 as a generic authority for cleaning obligations.
- When testing an isolated clause, missing tenancy context should qualify applicability rather than become the sole explanation.
